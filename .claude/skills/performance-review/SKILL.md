# Performance Review

This skill helps a manager produce a review that is fair, evidence-based, and consistent with every other review written in this company. Consistency is the entire point: reviews feed compensation and promotion decisions, they may be read years later in disputes, and two managers describing the same performance must land on the same rating. That's why this skill draws every criterion, rating definition, and section heading from the company's own documents — never from general knowledge about what performance reviews usually contain.

## Source of truth — read these first, every time

Before doing anything else, read all three reference files:

- `references/competencies.md` — the competencies to score, with the company's definition of each
- `references/ratings.md` — the rating scale and the exact definition of each rating level
- `references/template.md` — the review document structure, section by section

**If any of these files still contains the marker `HR-PLACEHOLDER`, stop.** Tell the manager the framework documents haven't been installed yet, name which file(s) are placeholders, and explain that you can't score or draft without them — substituting a generic framework would produce a review that doesn't match the company's calibration and could not be defended later. Offer to help them (or HR) paste the real content into the files; each placeholder file describes the expected format. You may still help with step 1 (gathering input) while the framework is missing, since collecting evidence doesn't depend on it.

Two rules that follow from these files being the source of truth:

- **Quote rating definitions verbatim.** When you propose or record a rating, quote its full definition from `references/ratings.md`, word for word, in quotation marks. Don't paraphrase, summarize, or "improve" the wording — managers calibrate against the exact text, and a paraphrase is how two managers end up meaning different things by "Meets Expectations."
- **Never add, rename, merge, or drop criteria.** Score exactly the competencies in `references/competencies.md` — all of them, and only them. If the manager asks you to rate something that isn't in the framework ("culture fit", "executive presence", "attitude"), decline to score it as a competency and explain why: unframework'd criteria are where inconsistency and bias enter reviews. The manager can put such observations in a template section that allows free-form comments, if one exists — but flag that behavioral evidence, not personality labels, belongs there too.

## Step 1 — Gather input

A review is only as good as its evidence. Before any scoring, help the manager assemble what exists. Ask which of these they have, and collect what they can share:

- The employee's self-review, if the cycle includes one
- Peer / stakeholder feedback collected this cycle
- Goals or objectives set at the start of the period, and what happened to each
- The manager's own 1:1 notes and running observations
- Concrete work products, metrics, or outcomes attributable to the employee
- The previous review, for trajectory (not for anchoring the new ratings)

Two failure modes to actively counter while gathering:

- **Recency bias.** Ask specifically for examples from the *early* part of the review period. If everything the manager remembers happened in the last six weeks, say so and prompt them to check their notes and the goal list from the start of the cycle.
- **Vague impressions.** "She's great with clients" is an impression; "the Meridian renewal, where the client asked that she lead the QBR" is evidence. When the manager offers an impression, ask for the incident behind it. Every rating will need at least one concrete example — better to surface them now than to discover a gap while drafting.

Keep confidentiality boundaries: don't ask for, and gently decline, other employees' ratings or comparative details ("just make it like Sarah's review") — comparisons happen in calibration sessions, not inside one person's review.

## Step 2 — Score against the competencies

Work through `references/competencies.md` one competency at a time, in the order listed:

1. Show the competency's name and the company's definition of it (from the file — verbatim).
2. Ask the manager which evidence from step 1 bears on this competency, and add anything they volunteer.
3. Propose a rating **only when the evidence supports one**, quoting the full verbatim definition of the proposed level from `references/ratings.md` and stating, in one or two sentences, how the evidence meets that definition — and, where relevant, why it doesn't meet the definition one level up.
4. If the evidence is too thin to distinguish between two levels, say exactly that and ask for more, rather than defaulting to the middle of the scale. "I don't have enough evidence to place this" is a legitimate and useful output; a guessed rating is not.
5. Let the manager decide. The manager owns the rating; your job is to make the evidence-to-definition reasoning explicit so their decision is defensible.

Keep a running scorecard as you go: competency, rating, and the one or two pieces of evidence that anchor it. You'll need it for the draft.

## Step 3 — Draft in the template

Open `references/template.md` and follow it exactly:

- Use its sections, in its order, with its headings — no sections added or removed, even if a section feels redundant for this employee. (An empty-feeling section gets a brief honest entry, not deletion.)
- Where the template calls for a rating, write the rating name and, if the template includes definitions, the verbatim definition text.
- Write in specific, behavioral language: what the person did, what happened as a result. Avoid personality adjectives ("brilliant", "difficult", "not a team player") — they're unfalsifiable and they're where bias lives. If a personality word survives into the draft, replace it with the incident that prompted it.
- Development areas get the same evidence standard as strengths: a specific observed gap, its impact, and a concrete suggested next step — not a softened compliment.

## Step 4 — Review the draft before handing it over

Run these checks and tell the manager the result of each:

1. **Framework check** — every scored item appears in `references/competencies.md`; nothing else got scored.
2. **Verbatim check** — every rating definition quoted in the draft matches `references/ratings.md` exactly.
3. **Evidence check** — every rating and every development area has at least one concrete example attached; flag any that rest on impressions alone.
4. **Balance check** — evidence spans the whole review period, not just recent weeks.
5. **Language check** — no personality labels, no comparisons to named colleagues, no promises the manager can't make alone (promotions, raises).

Remind the manager of two things at the end: the draft is an input to their judgment, not a substitute for it — they should read it as the employee will; and ratings may shift in calibration, so they shouldn't share the draft with the employee before calibration completes.
