Fla. Stat. §430.5025 · Complete guide

Florida ADRD Training Requirements: Who Needs What, By When

By Danielle Hammerstone, RN, BSN — former Director of Nursing · Verified against the current statute · Updated August 2026

Every Florida nursing home, home health agency, nurse registry, homemaker/companion provider, health care services pool, assisted living facility, adult family-care home, and adult day care center is a “covered provider” under Florida Statutes §430.5025 — and every one of them owes specific Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) training to specific workers on specific deadlines.

This guide lays out the full matrix: which requirement reaches which worker, when each is due, what record proves it, and what AHCA is actually citing. It's written from the statute, not from a vendor's summary of it.

1. The two definitions everything turns on

Every requirement in the statute keys off two terms. Get these wrong on your roster and every downstream answer is wrong too.

“Personal care” means in-person assistance with activities of daily living, self-administration of medication, homemaker or companion services, nursing services, or other services promoting well-being. It excludes administrative work and maintaining the physical environment — grounds, building maintenance, housekeeping, laundry, and food preparation.

“Regular contact” means duties other than personal care that may require daily in-person interaction with participants, patients, or residents.

The classification trap

A scheduler who visits clients weekly, an office manager who covers shifts, a cook who chats with residents daily — these are the arguable cases, and they're where audits go wrong. The requirements and deadlines differ by classification, so if your roster doesn't record a decision for every worker, you cannot prove compliance for any of the arguable ones. Where a case is genuinely unclear, document the administrator's determination — don't leave it implicit.

3. In-home providers: home health agencies, nurse registries, homemaker/companion

Three requirements reach your workforce:

  1. Written information for everyone, on hire. Every employee — including office staff — must receive basic written ADRD information when they start. The proof is an acknowledgment in the personnel file.
  2. The 1-hour DOEA training within 30 days for anyone providing personal care or having regular contact. This training must come from the Department of Elder Affairs. A vendor course, however good, does not satisfy it.
  3. 2 additional hours within 7 months for personal-care workers, covering behavior management, promoting independence in daily living, and working with families and caregivers.

Nurse registries: yes, this means you

The statute's definition of “employee” includes contracted staff and independent contractors who are employed or referred by a covered provider. Referring rather than employing a caregiver does not remove the requirement — and the citations issued so far have been against nurse registries specifically.

4. Facilities: nursing homes, ALFs, adult family-care homes, adult day care

Same structure, one difference: personal-care workers owe 3 additional hours within 7 months instead of 2, covering behavior management, activities, environmental issues, and ethical care. The written-information and 1-hour DOEA requirements apply identically.

5. Specialized dementia settings

An ALF, adult family-care home, or adult day care center that advertises or holds itself out as providing specialized Alzheimer's or dementia care carries a heavier, faster set:

  • 3 hours within 3 months — and this is the same 3-hour training from §4(d), pulled forward from 7 months and extended to regular-contact staff, not a separate additional course. One 3-hour obligation, not two.
  • 4 dementia-specific hours within 6 months for personal-care workers.
  • At least 4 hours of continuing education every calendar year thereafter — the only requirement in the statute that recurs annually, and therefore the one that silently lapses.

6. The July 2026 transition deadline

This date has passed

Anyone employed, contracted, or referred to provide services before July 1, 2023 had to complete all required training by July 1, 2026, unless qualifying equivalent training applies. For long-tenured staff, there is no runway left: a missing record for a pre-2023 worker is not “in progress” — it is overdue.

This is the population most audits find problems in, for a mundane reason: workers hired years ago predate whatever tracking system you use now, and their files were built under different management, different software, or no system at all.

7. What AHCA is citing: tag CZ875

AHCA assigns deficiency tag CZ875 for ADRD training non-compliance. As of 2026, at least five Florida nurse registries have been cited under it. The documented failures were not exotic:

  1. No written ADRD information provided at the time of hire
  2. No proof of the 1-hour DOEA training within 30 days
  3. No proof of the 2-hour training within 7 months for personal-care staff

Notice what these have in common: each is a record. The question a surveyor asks is not “did you train your people?” — it is “show me.” A trained workforce with an empty file fails the same way an untrained one does.

8. The records that count as proof

  • Written information: a signed acknowledgment, or policy-based evidence that the material is provided at start of employment — dated, and matched to the worker.
  • 1-hour DOEA training: the DOEA completion record or certificate with the worker's name and completion date.
  • Additional and specialized hours: a completion record showing provider, topics, hours, date, and setting.
  • Annual CE (specialized settings): records by topic, date, and hours for each calendar year.
  • Pre-2023 staff relying on equivalency: the completion or equivalency evidence actually relied on — not an assumption that “they must have done it at some point.”

Three failure modes account for most gaps: the record exists but can't be found; the record exists but is missing a required element (no date, no provider name, wrong worker); and the record never existed because the worker's classification was never decided. Only the first is quick to fix.

9. Common questions

Can we use our own training instead of the DOEA 1-hour?

No. The 1-hour program must be the one provided by the Department of Elder Affairs. Everything else in the matrix can be delivered by an approved provider or qualifying pathway; the 1-hour cannot be substituted.

Our caregivers are 1099 contractors. Does this apply?

Yes. The statute's definition of employee covers contracted staff and independent contractors employed or referred by a covered provider.

What about office staff who never see clients?

They still receive the written information on hire — that requirement reaches every employee. The 1-hour and additional-hours requirements only reach personal care and regular contact. Document the classification either way.

We do specialized dementia care. Is the 3-hour due twice?

No — §4(e)1 requires “the additional 3 hours of training as provided in paragraph (d).” Same training, faster clock (3 months, not 7), wider population (regular contact included). One obligation.

Is a worker compliant if they did the training but we can't find the certificate?

For survey purposes, a record you cannot produce does not protect you. The practical fix is usually re-obtaining the completion record from the training provider — start there before re-training anyone.

No. It's a practitioner's summary of the current statute, verified against the statutory text at the date shown above. Requirements can change; check the current law, and take genuinely disputed classification questions to counsel.
